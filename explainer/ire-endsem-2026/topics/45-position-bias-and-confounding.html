Lesson 45 / 69 · Ranking & signals

Position bias and confounding

Higher-ranked items get more clicks because they receive more attention, not necessarily because they are better.

High yieldProvided module reference

Basis: M3 p.30. Page labels point to the provided files or named reading; the explanation and exercises here are independently written.

01 / Understand

What actually happens?

Observed CTR mixes examination and attractiveness. Randomly moving the same item across positions reveals the examination curve. Training on raw clicks without correcting for exposure locks in previous ranking choices.

Core principle
Higher-ranked items get more clicks because they receive more attention, not necessarily because they are better.

Try to reason through the mechanism

  1. Identify what the system is trying to preserve or measure in the situation.
  2. Separate the observed symptom from the cause described in this lesson.
  3. Choose the operation, model or trade-off that fits that cause; name its failure mode.
02 / Small example

A concrete case

A fixed item shown at rank 1 gets more clicks than when shown at rank 7 even with identical content.

Equation or decision rule

P(click|d,k)≈P(examine|k)·P(attractive|d)

Know what each symbol or action means. The instructor's announced final-exam format emphasizes applying principles to short objective scenarios.

03 / Examiner's trap

A tempting answer that fails

Interpreting the higher CTR at top ranks as pure relevance.

Use an explicit four-part answer: symptom → principle → mechanism → consequence. A strong answer explains why the apparently easy choice is unsafe or incomplete.

04 / Active recall

Exam-style scenario

The same result loses CTR when randomly moved lower. Which explanation fits?
One-line viva / short-answer question

How can the examination bias be estimated?

Randomized position swaps or controlled exposure experiments.
