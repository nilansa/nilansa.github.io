Goodman's p-value misconception checklist

A p-value is neither a hypothesis posterior nor a business decision rule.

High yieldInstructor-directed reading

Basis: Goodman (2008) p.136 table 1; Anil's linked reading. Page labels point to the provided files or named reading; the explanation and exercises here are independently written.

01 / Understand

What actually happens?

The reading lists twelve recurring mistakes, including treating p=0.05 as a 5% chance H0 is true, calling p>0.05 'no effect', equating significance with importance, and treating threshold-crossing studies as contradictory. A decision requires effect size, uncertainty, context and replication.

Core principle
A p-value is neither a hypothesis posterior nor a business decision rule.

Try to reason through the mechanism

  1. Identify what the system is trying to preserve or measure in the situation.
  2. Separate the observed symptom from the cause described in this lesson.
  3. Choose the operation, model or trade-off that fits that cause; name its failure mode.
02 / Small example

A concrete case

Two otherwise similar studies report p=0.049 and 0.051; their evidence is usually much closer than a binary label suggests.

Equation or decision rule

p-value is conditional on H0, not P(H0|data)

Know what each symbol or action means. The instructor's announced final-exam format emphasizes applying principles to short objective scenarios.

03 / Examiner's trap

A tempting answer that fails

Treating 0.049 and 0.051 as radically different facts.

Use an explicit four-part answer: symptom → principle → mechanism → consequence. A strong answer explains why the apparently easy choice is unsafe or incomplete.

04 / Active recall

Exam-style scenario

Two tests estimate nearly identical positive lifts; one has p=0.049 and the other p=0.051. Best conclusion?
One-line viva / short-answer question

Give two common p-value misconceptions.

'p=0.05 means H0 has 5% probability' and 'p>0.05 proves no difference' are both false.
