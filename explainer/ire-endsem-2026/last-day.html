PRIORITY 01

Pick a ranking metric

A support search must put the correct password-reset article first. A shopping search should put several useful products on the first screen.

MRR = mean(1 ÷ rank of first relevant result). nDCG@k = DCG@k ÷ ideal DCG@k. For this guide, DCG = Σ(2^grade − 1) ÷ log₂(rank + 1).

Work it through. Grades [0, 2, 0, 2]: at rank 1 the gain is 0. At rank 2 it is 3 ÷ log₂3. IDCG sorts the same judged items by grade and uses the same cutoff. Dividing by IDCG makes the score relative to the best possible ordering for that query.

Exam rule. Use MRR for the first useful answer. Use nDCG when several results and grades matter. Use recall@k for candidates: an item dropped upstream cannot be recovered by reranking.

PRIORITY 02

Learn what to move up

You already know SGD: a loss tells you how to change a model. Changing the loss changes which ranking errors receive the most work.

For a preferred A over B, let d = score(A) − score(B). Pairwise logistic loss is log(1 + exp(−d)).

Work it through. If d = −1, the loss is about 1.31. If d = +1, it is about 0.31. The derivative −1/(1 + exp(d)) pushes the winning score up and the losing score down. A LambdaRank-style update scales that pair’s push by |ΔnDCG|. With grades [0,2,0,2], swaps 1↔2 and 3↔4 each fix one inversion, but the nDCG gains are about 0.226 and 0.043.

Exam rule. Pointwise fits items separately. Pairwise learns an order. A metric-weighted pair update cares where the error occurs. Do not trade away the candidate set to improve the loss on items you already found.

PRIORITY 03

Separate exposure from relevance

A rank-1 result has twice as many clicks as a rank-6 result. Users may like it more, or they may just see it more often.

Under a simple examination model: P(click) ≈ P(examined) × P(click | examined). The bar means “given that”: the second term asks what fraction clicked among the people who actually looked.

Work it through. A card shown at the top is examined 80% of the time and clicked 25% of those times: CTR = 20%. At the bottom it is examined 40% of the time: CTR = 10%, with no change in appeal. Randomized exposure can help estimate the examination difference.

Exam rule. An unclicked item may be unseen. Raw click logs reflect the old ranker. Check feature timestamps too: a future click count cannot be an input at serving time.

PRIORITY 04

Design an A/B test

A new ranker boosts click-through but may slow responses. The same account can open several sessions.

Choose the randomization unit before rollout. State the primary outcome, guardrails, duration and analysis unit.

Work it through. Assign each account once, compare account-level outcomes, and monitor p95 latency and conversion. Shared inventory can let treatment change control outcomes; consider a cluster design if it does and account for fewer independent units. Interleaving can compare preference quickly, but it is not a substitute for a conversion A/B test.

Exam rule. When both rankers score the same queries offline, use paired differences. When distinct randomized users see A and B online, compare the two groups at the appropriate unit.

PRIORITY 05

Read the result without overclaiming

Control conversion is 10.0%; treatment is 10.3%. The 95% interval for the difference is [−0.2, +0.8] percentage points.

Effect = +0.3 percentage points, or +3% relative. Approximate planning rule at fixed alpha and power: n ∝ variance ÷ MDE².

Work it through. The interval includes zero, harm and benefit: you have not shown equivalence. Halving the minimum detectable effect needs about 4× the sample size. If A yields p=.04 and B yields p=.06, test the difference directly; a threshold crossing in one arm does not establish a difference.

Exam rule. A p-value measures how surprising a result at least this extreme is under the null and test assumptions. It is not the probability that the null is true. Do not stop at the first p<.05 without a valid sequential rule. Report the effect and interval. Define one primary metric up front; adjust or clearly label exploratory comparisons.

15 MIN · 6 MCQs + 1 CHALLENGE

Mini-mock A

Pick one answer. Say why in one line. Give yourself two points per MCQ and four for the challenge. This is a practice scale, not the final’s marking scheme.

  1. A help centre needs one correct setup link quickly. Which metric fits?

    A. MRR   ·   B. recall@100   ·   C. average latency

    Show answer

    MRR. It rewards the rank of the first relevant link.

  2. The exact reranker never sees the best document. What should you improve first?

    A. Reranker loss   ·   B. candidate recall   ·   C. calibration

    Show answer

    Candidate recall. A reranker cannot score a document that was never retrieved.

  3. A is preferred to B, but score(A)−score(B) is negative. What should SGD do to this gap?

    A. Increase   ·   B. decrease   ·   C. preserve it

    Show answer

    Increase it. The pairwise logistic loss falls as the preferred item moves ahead.

  4. Identical cards get more clicks in a higher slot. What is the strongest conclusion?

    A. Higher appeal   ·   B. higher observed clicks   ·   C. higher conversion

    Show answer

    Higher observed clicks. Exposure and appeal are confounded without more evidence.

  5. The same query set is scored using both rankers. How should you bootstrap the difference?

    A. Draw individual documents   ·   B. draw paired queries   ·   C. draw both arms independently

    Show answer

    Draw paired queries. Keep both scores together for each independent query.

  6. A test has p=.18 and a wide interval. Is it evidence the effect is zero?

    A. Yes   ·   B. no   ·   C. only if the sample is large

    Show answer

    No. A wide interval may contain meaningful effects in both directions.

  7. Challenge: You can fix one pair. Swapping the top pair adds .18 nDCG; swapping the lower pair adds .02. Both pairwise losses match. What changes when you use a metric-weighted pair update?

    Show answer

    Weight the first swap 9× as much. The absolute metric change sets the weight; .18/.02 = 9.

ORIGINAL PRACTICE · LECTURE-BACKED

Hypothesis testing: three decision rules

  1. Same queries, two rankers: use within-query score differences, not two unrelated samples. Pairing removes some query-to-query noise.
  2. Half the minimum detectable lift: roughly 4× as much data, holding variance, significance level and power fixed. Variance in a heavy-tailed money metric is a separate reason to need more data.
  3. Non-significant with a wide interval: inconclusive, not “no impact.” A p-value is about data under the null, not the probability the null is true. Report the estimated difference and interval.
Practice: both tests give p = .04; did they measure the same lift?

No. Effect and standard error can differ. Compare the estimates and intervals in the original metric units. This is a practice scenario based on the assigned Goodman reading, not a past question.

ORIGINAL PRACTICE · CONCEPT-SHEET GAPS

Three online-experiment scenarios

  1. Interference / SUTVA: The new ranker uses more CPU on shared servers, slowing down control requests. Does randomizing individual users fix it?

    Show answer

    No. Control outcomes depend on treatment assignment. Randomize isolated server pools or use time blocks with a transition window. Analyze at the randomized unit; fewer independent units can reduce power.

  2. Sample-ratio mismatch: A planned 50/50 split produced 58/42 among analyzed users. Should you announce a lift?

    Show answer

    Not yet. Check assignment, eligibility and logging separately by arm; filters or missing events can also cause a mismatch. Diagnose before interpreting effects.

  3. Interleaving: Ranker B wins clicks when its results are mixed into the same list as A’s. Can you conclude it increases purchases?

    Show answer

    No. Interleaving can screen for ranking preference; a separate A/B test should measure conversions and guardrails under each full experience.

Bonus: two retrieval systems have incomparable scores and miss different items

Combine candidates and fuse by rank or calibrate scores before combining. A late ranker cannot recover an item excluded from every candidate list. An abstention rule needs a threshold; requesting top-k alone returns k even if the best results are bad.

First three: secure the early-topic marks

1. Load, latency and serving

L = λW. At fixed arrival rate, longer response time means more in-flight work. Near saturation, queueing and the slowest shard can dominate p99. Think: bottleneck → consequence → fix.

Past-paper evidence: Quiz 2 Q1, Q2, Q4 and Q19(a); Quiz 1 Q1(b).

  1. At 180 requests/s, average response time rises from 50 ms to 100 ms. CPU looks flat. Which pool is now under more pressure?

    A. Connection/worker pool · B. Vocabulary table · C. Neither, if CPU is flat

    Show answer

    The connection/worker pool: average in-flight work goes from 9 to 18. The arrival rate is unchanged, so each request holds a slot twice as long.

  2. A service at 95% utilization hedges every slow request. Give one reason p99 might get worse.

    Show answer

    Extra copies add work to replicas that are already busy, raising queueing; hedge selectively only with headroom or a separate pool.

2. Storage, partitioning and consistency

Pick the invariant first. Stale catalog results may be acceptable, but a partition cannot be allowed to cause duplicate charges. Bloom filters buy cheaper negative reads at the cost of memory; RUM trade-offs move work among reads, updates and space.

Past-paper evidence: Quiz 1 Q1(a,c), Quiz 2 Q9 and Q13, and the midsem key’s sketch reference panel.

  1. Two regions lose contact. Search can show a stale listing, but the same wallet must not be debited twice. Which design is safest?

    A. Serve both everywhere · B. Refuse both everywhere · C. Serve search; require authority for debit

    Show answer

    Keep serving stale listings if allowed, but reject or defer the debit where the authority needed to prevent duplicate spends is unavailable.

  2. A Bloom filter on an immutable segment says “absent.” Can you skip disk access? What if it says “present”?

    Show answer

    Absent: skip, assuming a correctly built filter. Present: check the segment; false positives are possible.

3. Text processing and lexical scoring

Index and query must use the same analyzer. BM25 rewards rarity, saturates repeated term matches and discounts extra length. BM25F combines weighted field evidence before applying saturation.

Past-paper evidence: Quiz 2 Q14 and Q15 directly test text/index behavior; BM25 is in Module 3 and the concept sheet, not a verified Quiz 2 calculation.

  1. The index stores “running” as “run,” but the live query analyzer is changed to keep “running” unstemmed. What is the likely effect?

    A. Better recall by default · B. Missing matches · C. Only a calibration change

    Show answer

    Recall can drop because indexed and query terms no longer match; restore analyzer parity or rebuild the index with compatible processing.

  2. Write the BM25 length factor B and explain what happens to one term’s score when length rises at fixed tf and b > 0.

    Show answer

    B = 1−b+b|d|/avgdl; a longer document raises B. The denominator tf+k₁B grows, so contribution falls (for positive k₁ and IDF).

  1. A response calls 12 parallel services and waits for all of them. A redesign uses 70, each slightly faster. Why can p99 rise?

    Answer and why: The response depends on the slowest service, and more opportunities for a straggler can outweigh the faster median. Inspect fan-out and tail latencies.

    Know: Tail at scale: a max, not an average. Add deadlines with partial results if the product can tolerate them.

  2. At 150 requests/s and 120 ms mean response time, how many requests are in the system? Do you plug in p99 instead?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: 18 on average: 150 × .120. No, the formula uses the mean over the same system boundary.

    Know: Little’s law N=X×R. Units must cancel correctly.

ORIGINAL MINI-MOCK · 2 OF 8

Storage, partitioning and consistency

Evidence for practicing: Quiz 1 Q1(a,c), Quiz 2 Q9 and Q13, Module 2 sketches.

  1. A rare token is definitely absent from 49 of 50 segments. Why is a Bloom filter more useful for it than for a token present in every segment?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: Negative responses skip most segment lookups; a false positive only costs a check. A common token makes every filter report present, so none of the segments can be skipped.

    Know: Bloom filters spend RAM to save reads; false positives are possible, false negatives are not for a correct standard filter.

  2. Two partitioned regions can each update the same account balance. Search can be an hour stale. Which operation must fail closed when its authority is unavailable?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: The balance change, to preserve the money invariant. Search can keep serving stale results if the product allows it.

    Know: CAP is a trade-off under partition, and policies can differ by operation.

ORIGINAL MINI-MOCK · 3 OF 8

Text processing and lexical scoring

Evidence for practicing: Quiz 2 Q14–Q16; Module 2 analysis and Module 3 BM25.

  1. Query time strips the hyphen from an error code, but indexing did not. Does increasing the code’s IDF repair it?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: No. The query token may never meet its posting list. Align analysis at index and query time first.

    Know: Matching comes before scoring; IDF cannot boost a term that never matches.

  2. A product title contains ‘earbuds’ once and the body twice. In the simplified lecture example, title weight is 3 and body weight is 1. What does BM25F saturate?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: One combined count, 3×1+1×2=5, rather than saturating the title and body separately.

    Know: BM25F combines field evidence before the nonlinear TF step; ordinary BM25 does not assign these field weights.

ORIGINAL MINI-MOCK · 4 OF 8

Ranking and re-ranking

Evidence for practicing: Announced final scope and Module 3 cascade, objectives and rankers.

  1. Lexical retrieves relevant A and B, semantic retrieves B and C. You can afford both, and no query feature tells you which misses what. Route or fuse?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: Fuse. The union contains A, B and C, while either single route misses a relevant item. Use ranks or calibrated scores to combine.

    Know: Complementary retrievers can improve candidate recall; a later reranker cannot retrieve omitted items.

  2. Two equally hard pairwise errors have |ΔnDCG| of .16 and .04. What changes with the metric-weighted update?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: The first pair gets four times the metric multiplier. Plain pairwise loss does not explicitly account for the position impact.

    Know: RankNet learns pair order; LambdaRank weights updates by metric impact.

Signals, bias and leakage

Evidence for practicing: Module 3 slides 27–32 and 38; Nov 2 logs and bias recordings.

  1. An identical item is examined 75% of the time at the top and 25% lower down. If 40% of those who examine click, what CTRs do you expect?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: 30% and 10%. Only examination changed; attractiveness stayed .40.

    Know: Position-model CTR≈examination×attractiveness; attractiveness is the conditional click probability after a look.

  2. The training feature ‘clicks in the last hour’ includes clicks 20 minutes after the scored request. Is that weak calibration or leakage?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: Leakage. Reconstruct the feature using events before the scored request, with the same cutoff logic as serving.

    Know: Same feature recipe, different timestamps; future information is unavailable online.

ORIGINAL MINI-MOCK · 6 OF 8

Offline evaluation

  1. One ranker’s top 10 are mostly judged; another’s are largely unjudged. Your metric counts unjudged as nonrelevant. What should accompany their scores?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: Show judged coverage for both and be cautious about concluding the second is worse. Expand the pool if possible.

    Know: Pooling limits what evaluation knows; unjudged is not the same as known irrelevant.

  2. Two judges agree 80% of the time; chance agreement from their label frequencies is 60%. What is Cohen’s κ?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: (.80−.60)/(1−.60)=.50: the judges close half the gap between chance and perfect agreement.

ORIGINAL MINI-MOCK · 7 OF 8

Hypothesis tests

Evidence for practicing: Nov 12 course recording and the assigned Goodman reading; part of A/B scope.

  1. The p-value is .04. Does that mean a 4% probability that no effect exists?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: No. If the null and test assumptions hold, .04 is the probability of a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one. Compare with a prespecified threshold and report the effect and interval.

    Know: The p-value conditions on the null, not the other way around. It is not an effect size.

  2. The metric’s standard deviation doubles and the minimum detectable effect is halved. Other settings stay fixed. What happens to required n?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: Roughly 16×: variance becomes 4×, and dividing by the squared halved effect adds another 4×.

    Know: At fixed significance and power, n is proportional to variance divided by squared detectable effect.

ORIGINAL MINI-MOCK · 8 OF 8

Online experiments

Evidence for practicing: Announced A/B scope; concept sheet §8 for explicit design failure modes.

  1. Treatment and control share the same queue. Treatment raises requests per user and slows controls. Would a user-level split fix this?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: No. Isolate pools or randomize time blocks with washout; analyze at the randomized unit.

    Know: Interference means one unit’s treatment affects another unit’s outcome (the no-interference part of SUTVA).

  2. A 50/50 test reports 55/45 among analyzed users and looks positive. What comes before the significance test?

    Answer, why it works, and concept

    Answer and why: Check the assignment and exposure logs, eligibility filters and missing results by arm. Fix or account for the mismatch before interpreting lift.

    Know: Sample-ratio mismatch is a validity warning. It is not a business outcome or evidence of treatment benefit.

Worked practice for the sticking points

Answer before opening. If you are short on time, start with questions 1, 3, 4 and 6.

  1. Work in flight: Throughput is 125 requests/s. Mean latency is 80 ms; p99 is 800 ms. How many are in flight, and why is 100 the wrong answer?

    Show worked answer

    10: 125×.080. A p99 of .800 describes an upper percentile, so 125×.800 is not Little’s law’s average.

  2. Why length and fields are different: Two documents contain a term twice. One is at half the average length and the other twice average. With b=.5, calculate B, then say which scores higher at the same IDF and k₁>0.

    Show worked answer

    B_short=.75; B_long=1.50. The shorter document has the smaller denominator and scores higher at equal positive IDF. BM25F is a separate choice: it can give the title more weight before TF saturates.

  3. Clicks vs. propensity: CTR is 16% at the top and 4% lower down for the same randomized item. If top examination is .8, what would lower examination be?

    Show worked answer

    Relative examination is .04/.16=.25, so .8×.25=.20. A lower-slot click would count as 1/.20=5 under inverse propensity weighting. Without a top-slot anchor you know only the ratio.

  4. Calibration direction: Keep all clicks and one in five non-clicks. The sample CTR is 50%. Compute real CTR and say why it moves that way.

    Show worked answer

    0.50/(0.50+0.50/.20)=1/6≈16.7%. The sample overrepresents clicks. Restore the dropped non-click weight, rather than shrinking it again.

  5. Decide whether to rerank or fuse: A keyword retriever gets A and B, semantic gets B and C, and all three are relevant to one query. The reranker sees only A and B. Why won’t a stronger model return C?

    Show worked answer

    C never reached it. Fuse the two sources or expand candidate generation. Then rerank. A reranker’s precision is conditional on what the candidate stage handed it.

  6. Two p-value traps: A metric doubles its standard deviation and the team wants to detect the same effect. Then p=.12 with a wide interval. What changes, and what does the outcome establish?

    Show worked answer

    Plan for roughly 4× data if α, power and target effect are fixed. p=.12 with a wide interval is inconclusive; it does not prove zero effect or give a 12% chance of no effect.

  7. Which experiment flaw?: A planned 50/50 test ends 58/42 after filtering, and a shared queue slows controls. What are the two distinct problems?

    Show worked answer

    The split is sample-ratio mismatch; audit assignment and filters. The shared queue is interference; isolate pools or choose appropriate randomized clusters or blocks. A significance test does not repair either.
