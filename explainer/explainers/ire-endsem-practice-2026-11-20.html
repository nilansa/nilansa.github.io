Precision and recall: two denominators, two questions

Suppose a search engine shows 8 papers. Five are relevant; the collection contains 12 relevant papers in total. Precision = 5/8 = 62.5%: of what we showed, what fraction was useful? Recall = 5/12 ≈ 41.7%: of what was useful, what fraction did we find? There are 3 false positives and 7 false negatives.

Retrieved = TP + FP → denominator for precision.

Relevant = TP + FN → denominator for recall.

Imagine the next four retrieved papers contain one relevant paper. At 12 results, precision is 6/12 = 50% and recall is 6/12 = 50%. With a fixed ranked list and fixed relevance judgments, recall cannot fall when you increase the cutoff, but precision can rise, fall, or stay flat. When ranking systems against one another, fix the cutoff and the relevance judgments first.

F1 balances both: 2PR/(P+R), or 2TP/(2TP+FP+FN). For the first eight results it is 10/(10+3+7) = 0.50. F1 is not accuracy: it does not include true negatives. If a denominator is zero, check the convention rather than silently treating the ratio as defined.

Active recall: What if the engine returns every document?

If any relevant documents exist, recall is 1. Precision is simply the prevalence of relevance in the collection. This is why maximizing recall alone can produce a useless search interface.

Active recall: Search A has 4 relevant in 5; B has 6 relevant in 10. There are 10 relevant total. Which is better?

A has higher precision (0.80 vs 0.60); B has higher recall (0.60 vs 0.40). They use different cutoffs, so compare them at the same cutoff if your actual product shows a fixed number of results.

Ranking: choose the metric that matches the experience

Use P@k for the fraction of the first k results that are relevant. Use reciprocal rank when the first relevant result is what matters and AP when finding multiple relevant results early matters. Average RR or AP across queries for MRR or MAP. With graded relevance, nDCG rewards putting more useful documents higher. Individual metrics here are study recommendations, not a guaranteed list of exam questions.

Worked problem: binary labels [0, 1, 1, 0, 1] with four relevant documents total.

P@3 = 2/3; recall@3 = 2/4 = 0.5; RR = 1/2. With the list ending at rank 5 and the fourth relevant document unretrieved, AP = (1/2 + 2/3 + 3/5 + 0)/4 ≈ 0.442. Do not divide by the three relevant documents found: that would fail to penalize the omitted document.

Worked problem: nDCG@3 for grades [1, 3, 0].

Using gain 2rel−1 and discount log2(i+1), DCG = 1 + 7/log2(3) ≈ 5.417. Assuming these are all candidate grades, ideal [3,1,0] gives IDCG = 7 + 1/log2(3) ≈ 7.631. nDCG ≈ 0.710. If the metric specifies another gain function or there are better relevant items outside the retrieved results, adjust accordingly.

Choose the metric: users need the official sign-in page versus a thorough literature search.

For a sign-in lookup, choose reciprocal rank: users need one correct link quickly. For literature search, AP and recall at a usable depth can be informative; one good paper is not enough. If several results are helpful to different degrees, consider nDCG.

A/B testing: a lift is not automatically evidence

Define the question and primary outcome before looking. Randomly assign an appropriate unit, often the user when the same person makes repeated searches. Measure at the corresponding level or use an analysis that accounts for clustering. Check guardrails (such as latency) and report effect size and uncertainty, not only whether a threshold was crossed.

Worked problem: A has 180 successes in 1,000 users; B has 200 in 1,000. How big is the lift?

18% → 20% is +2 percentage points, or about +11.1% relative to A. Under an independent-user normal approximation, the standard error is about 1.75 percentage points and the 95% interval is roughly −1.4 to +5.4 percentage points. The observed lift is positive, but this interval includes zero. It does not establish equivalence or rule out a meaningful improvement.

Multiple choice: If p = 0.03, is there a 97% chance B is better?

No. Under the null model and test assumptions, p is the probability of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. It is not the probability the null is true, the chance of replication, or an effect size.

Scenario: every morning you check the result and stop the first time p < 0.05. What went wrong?

A fixed-horizon p-value does not retain its advertised false-positive rate under this stopping rule. Predefine the sample size or use a sequential test designed for repeated looks. Likewise, selecting a winning metric from many unadjusted tests after the fact turns confirmation into exploration.

What is confirmed, and how to study it

Confirmed scope: Quiz 2 material, plus Ranking and A/B testing. The exam announcement describes objective questions and a format similar to Quiz 2. The Quiz 2 guidance emphasized short scenarios and applying principles to the example; it excluded numerical questions for that quiz only. The calculations here develop intuition, not a claim about the final exam format or its formula sheet.

  1. First 25 minutes: Write precision and recall from memory. Explain which denominator changes when more results are retrieved, then solve the two search scenarios without notes.
  2. Next 30 minutes: Sort ranking metrics by user goal. Solve one AP and one nDCG example, then explain each in one sentence.
  3. Next 30 minutes: Practice choosing assignment units, recognizing invalid stopping rules, and interpreting a lift, confidence interval, and p-value in plain language.
  4. Last 15 minutes: Revisit the original Quiz 2 pre-reading and lecture notes for the pre-ranking material. For each principle, invent one realistic scenario and choose an answer before checking notes.

Public reference reading: Stanford’s Evaluation in Information Retrieval and Learning to Rank slides; Twelve P-Value Misconceptions, included with the course’s A/B-testing guidance. Examples on this page are original practice, not past or unreleased exam questions.

Quiz 2 foundations: reason from the failure

Use these as a study map, not as an official question list. The existing Quiz 2 guide has the longer pre-ranking sequence.

Queues and cost
Utilization is work demanded divided by capacity. Near saturation, variability can produce a large queue even when average demand is below capacity. Little’s law, L = λW, relates average in-flight work to throughput and average time in a stable system. Service demand is time using a resource, not time waiting for it.
Latency at scale
If a request needs every shard to finish, the slowest needed shard controls completion. Batch work prioritizes throughput; online serving also needs a latency target. Offline validation saves live risk; online outcomes reflect delivery and actual user behavior.
Consistency trade-offs
Read-your-writes asks for your update to be visible to your own later reads. CAP is about consistency versus availability during a network partition. PACELC adds the normal-time trade-off between latency and consistency. RUM helps you ask what read, update and memory costs a data structure trades off.
Bloom filters
A positive means “possibly present.” Check against the exact store before a consequential rejection. A negative means “absent” for the usual correctly maintained, insert-only filter.
Forty shard responses are required. Median performance is stable but the slowest responses worsen. What should you look at?

Look at tail latency of the request and its shards, not just the median. Taking the maximum of many shard times makes rare stragglers important.

Throughput stays at 100 requests/s. Mean response time rises from 0.10 s to 0.20 s. What happens to in-flight work?

At steady state, it doubles from 10 to 20 by Little’s law. This is an average, not a bound on a queue spike.

You edit a profile, then see the old name despite healthy replicas. Which guarantee would address this?

Read-your-writes. Route subsequent reads where your write is visible, or use a session mechanism that waits for an adequate version. CAP is not the immediate diagnosis; no partition was stated.

A Bloom filter flags a new signup as a duplicate. Reject it?

No. A positive may be false. Use an exact membership check.
