Statistical generalization, from first principles

From training loss to generalization

Precision and recall tell you how well a trained classifier did on the examples you measured. Generalization asks whether that result will carry over to new examples drawn from the same setting. Fix a loss first: for binary classification, 0–1 loss is one for an error and zero otherwise. Population risk is the expected loss on a fresh draw; empirical risk is the average loss over the sample you have. The generalization gap is population risk minus empirical risk. A small gap alone is not enough: a poor model can do equally badly on training and future data.

Unknown population → random sample → train or select a model → measure on fresh data from that population.

Example: A model gets zero training errors on 100 messages because it memorizes their IDs. On 100 independent new messages it misses about half. Its good training score says little about new messages. A rule that predicts from message content can have more training errors but lower true risk.

Why a fixed model and a selected model need different arguments

If you fix a classifier before looking at an independent test sample, its test error is an average of bounded random quantities. For 0–1 loss on independent, identically distributed examples, Hoeffding’s inequality implies a two-sided error bar of ±√[log(2/δ)/(2n)] at confidence 1−δ. With 1,000 examples and δ=.05 the bar is about ±4.3 percentage points. If you choose the best of 100 fixed candidates on that same sample, a union bound gives simultaneous error bars of ±√[log(200/δ)/(2n)], about ±6.4 points. This conservative bound protects all candidates at once, so it also protects whichever one the data selects. Reusing the test set to generate new candidates is a further adaptation; the 100-candidate calculation does not automatically apply.

Empirical risk minimization (ERM) chooses the lowest empirical loss in a class. If every candidate’s empirical risk lies within ε of its population risk, then the ERM choice’s true loss is at most 2ε above the best in that class: one ε to move from true risk to training risk for the winner, no increase when comparing training risks, and one ε back to true risk for the class optimum. The book-level question is how to justify that simultaneous guarantee without listing every model.

Two ways to measure what the model class can fit

  • VC dimension: ask for the largest number of points on which the class realizes every possible binary labeling. A one-sided threshold on a line can label one point either way, but cannot label two ordered points positive then negative; its VC dimension is one. The number of weights alone is not the definition.
  • Rademacher complexity: give the sample random plus/minus signs and ask how strongly the best function in a class can align with them, averaging over those signs. It measures a class’s ability to fit noise on a particular sample and extends to real-valued function and loss classes.

Practice: Which of 3% train error and 8% genuinely independent test error is an estimate of the risk of the model you trained? The 8%, provided the test sample represents deployment. If you change model choice after seeing those results, reserve a new final test set. If deployment differs from the test population, the usual same-distribution guarantee does not answer that new question.

Precision/recall caveat: they are ratios, not per-example bounded losses. Their uncertainty depends on how many predicted positives or actual positives support the denominator. The 0–1 error bar above does not transfer to either ratio just by replacing the metric name.

A whole-book route through the ideas

This is a reading route across the subject, not a claim about the unidentified book’s table of contents. Start with the first three steps. Use the rest as a map.

  1. Define the problem: What population do you want to predict on? Which examples did you draw? Which loss represents your mistakes?
  2. Make one reliable estimate: Show why averaging independent losses gives an estimate of risk, and why the uncertainty shrinks like 1/√n in the basic bounded-loss case.
  3. Allow for selection: A guarantee for one fixed classifier cannot protect whichever one looks best on the same sample. Use a finite-class bound first, then VC or Rademacher complexity when there are too many functions to count.
  4. Control what you can learn: Narrowing the class can reduce overfitting but exclude the true rule. A regularizer trades empirical fit for a preference such as smaller weights. Ask whether that preference matches your problem.
  5. Connect theory to tools: Margins and support vectors make the idea tangible: prefer a separator that does not barely squeeze past the data. Kernels change the features where that separator is simple. They do not guarantee good performance on their own.
  6. Check where the assumptions break: Correlated examples, shift, selection on test outcomes, and learning across tasks change which guarantee applies. Separate the scientific claim from what your data can actually validate.

A chapter you can use now: Chapter 11 of Foundations of Computer Vision starts with fitting noisy points by polynomials, then changes data, priors, and hypothesis space. Read that before a more abstract proof if the notation is getting in your way.

You have 1,000 test examples. Is adding 900 candidates or adding 1,000 examples more consequential here?

For the simple uniform bound, new candidates enter logarithmically, while n is in the denominator under the square root. With 100 fixed models, the ±6.4-point bound drops to about ±4.6 after doubling n. With 1,000 models and the original n, it rises to about ±7.3. These are worst-case bounds under the stated assumptions, not forecasts.
