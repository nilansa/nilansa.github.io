Can we test a new ranking system without deploying it?

01 / The question

Why are clicks not the same as relevance?

A search engine shows a ranked list. A user clicks some results. But a click is not a perfect relevance label. A result near the top is easier to see. A result near the bottom may be relevant, but the user may not look at it.

Research question: Can we use logs from an old ranking policy to estimate how a different ranking policy would perform? Can we train the new ranker from those logs?

There are three parts: evaluation (estimate the new ranker's quality), learning (train it from biased clicks), and guarantees (state when the estimate is valid and how uncertain it is).

02 / A story-based toy example

Maya changes the order of search results

Maya runs a book-search site. The old system puts Book A first and Book B second. A user clicks A. Did the user prefer A, or was A simply easier to see?

Toy illustration. It is not a real experiment.

Suppose the old system showed B at rank 1 in only 1 out of 4 similar visits. In that visit, the user clicked B. Its inverse-propensity weight is 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4. The rare exposure gets more weight.

IPS weight = 1 / probability of exposure = 1 / 0.25 = 4

If the old system never showed B at rank 1, that probability is zero. The log cannot tell us what would have happened. This is a support problem.

03 / The pipeline

From old logs to a safer ranking decision

1 · Query
User context and request.
→
2 · Old policy
Chooses and orders results.
→
3 · Feedback
Log items, positions and clicks.
→
4 · Correct bias
Use propensities or a reward model.
→
5 · New policy
Estimate value or train ranker.
Evaluation: old logs → off-policy estimator → score new ranker → uncertainty check → compare policies.
Learning: old logs → propensity model → weighted ranking loss → updated ranker → evaluate again.

The log should record the old policy's action probabilities, or enough data to estimate them. Clicks alone may not be enough.

04 / Main methods

Four ideas to compare

IPS. Give more weight to events that the old system rarely showed. This can correct bias. Very small probabilities create large weights and high variance.
Self-normalized IPS. Normalize the weights. This often reduces variance. It can add some finite-sample bias.
Doubly Robust. Combine a reward model with an IPS correction. Under the required assumptions, the estimate can remain consistent if either the reward model or propensity model is correct.
CRM. Do not select a policy only because its estimated reward is high. Also account for estimator variance. CRM uses a conservative performance bound.
Key assumptions: the new policy must have support in the logs; propensities must be known or estimated well; the reward must match the question; and the click process must be modelled carefully.
05 / Short history

How did the field develop?

  • 2010–2013 · Foundations. Contextual-bandit news recommendation showed how logged traffic can support offline evaluation. Doubly robust estimation combined reward models and propensity weighting. Counterfactual reasoning entered advertising.
  • 2015–2016 · Learning from logs. CRM added variance-aware learning. Self-normalized estimators improved stability. Recommendation research treated exposure as a selection process, not a neutral sample.
  • 2017–2018 · Unbiased ranking. Researchers developed counterfactual learning-to-rank, slate evaluation, and joint learning of a ranker and its propensity model.
  • 2019–2020 · Top-k and slates. Methods addressed general ranking metrics, top-k feedback, and ordered lists with sequential rewards.
  • 2021–2024 · More realistic assumptions. Work studied ranking-metric estimators, relevance and bias separation, and flexible click models that trade bias against variance.
