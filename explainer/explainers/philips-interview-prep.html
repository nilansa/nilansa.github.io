Philips Round 1

Applied ML practice for explaining your work, defending an experiment, and making a deployment decision.

Independent practice guide. The questions below are original examples, not an official interview format.

1. Run a 35-minute rehearsal

  1. 5 min · Introduce yourself. Rehearse a one-minute answer. Use the rest for follow-up questions.
  2. 8 min · Data selection. Explain your contribution and why the baseline is fair.
  3. 8 min · Retrieval and ranking. Explain why recall and ranking quality can disagree.
  4. 10 min · Validation case. State what evidence would change your deployment decision.
  5. 4 min · Your questions. Ask about the team, the problem and how success is measured.
35:00

2. Prepare two project stories

For each: problem → baseline → your contribution → measurement → trade-off. Use only figures you can verify and explain.

CPU-side data selection

What was expensive in the original pipeline? Explain how the selection signal works, why it might fail, and what CPU time, memory and training compute cost. Compare against uniform sampling under the same token and training budget.

Likely follow-up: “If your proxy is good at finding duplicates but poor at finding hard examples, what would you test first?”

Retrieval and ranking

Sketch candidate generation and reranking separately. Explain candidate Recall@K, then nDCG@10 or MRR for ranking. Name one failure mode and one ablation that separates the effect of retrieval from the effect of ranking.

Likely follow-up: “If you retrieve the right item but it ranks at position 30, where would you intervene?”

Open a 60-second introduction template

“I work on [problem] under [constraint]. My contribution was [specific part]. I compared it with [baseline] using [metric and split] and learned [trade-off]. I’ve also worked on [ranking example], where I owned [piece]. I’m interested in work where evaluation tells us when a model is ready to use.”

3. Answer before opening the rubric

You can train on only 10% of a corpus. How do you pick it?
  • Define the target metric and a uniform random baseline with equal tokens, steps, compute and seeds.
  • Propose a cheap relevance, diversity or gradient proxy. State why the proxy might fail and measure its CPU cost separately.
  • If you use ANN, measure neighbor recall versus exact search and profile latency and memory. Hold out data before tuning; report downstream quality and selection overhead together.
A reranker improves nDCG@10 but doubles p95. Would you ship?
  • Identify the latency budget and where the gains occur, including difficult and infrequent queries.
  • Hold the candidate set fixed to isolate ranking gains. Compare lower top-K reranking, caching or a smaller reranker on the same traffic slice.
  • Choose a canary threshold and rollback rule for quality and latency. Do not recommend release until the trade-off meets the agreed targets.
A model is good on pooled data but weak at a new hospital. What next?
  • Define the intended user and plausible failure harms. Check patient, site and time splits before diagnosing shift.
  • Evaluate relevant subgroup performance, calibration and uncertainty. Investigate differences in data collection and outcome labels.
  • Agree on acceptance thresholds, human review or abstention paths, and monitoring before considering deployment. This case is hypothetical.
Your gain disappears on a fresh split. What do you report?

State both observations. Audit possible leakage and tuning on the original split, rerun fair baselines, and use uncertainty intervals if supported. Explain which conclusion survives and which does not.

4. Score the rehearsal, not your chances

Score each from 0 to 2: clarity, ownership, baseline, evidence, limitations. Rework the lowest item before repeating the drill. A high score here is a practice target, not a hiring prediction.

Ask the interviewer

  • Which team is this role with, and what would the first project look like?
  • What baseline and validation evidence would make a prototype ready for the next stage?
