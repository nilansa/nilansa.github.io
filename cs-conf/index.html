Purpose

Find problems that have a real research gap, can be tested cheaply, and fit a suitable venue. Several ideas may run in parallel, but only claims supported by controlled experiments should become submission-ready papers.

Loop: venue → prior work → falsifiable question → simplest controlled test → audit → better test or pivot → abstract → paper → human approval → submission.

Each idea has a stable ID, a minimal experiment, and a stopping condition. All entries begin as untested hypotheses. This public page does not contain private datasets, unpublished measurements, credentials, or collaborator transcripts.

Research protocol

  1. Observe venue constraints. Record deadline, scope, required abstract, AI disclosure, page limit, dual-submission rule, reviewer or coauthor quotas, and costs.
  2. Identify novelty. Read the closest accepted papers, their methods and benchmarks, not only abstracts. Mark direct overlap and strong baselines.
  3. Formulate. Write objects, assumptions, empirical phenomenon, exact question, measurable metric, and one falsifiable claim. Do not confuse a prompt or intuition with a contribution.
  4. Precheck controls. Fix datasets, train/test split, seeds, evaluation code, baselines, total GPU-hours or inference tokens, and leak checks before running.
  5. Run the cheapest decisive pilot. First use released checkpoints, small models, synthetic controls, or mathematical counterexamples.
  6. Gate or pivot. Stop for known prior art, broken controls, data leakage, unstable gains, prohibitive cost, or an unsupported claim. Keep negative results in the ledger.
  7. Write as research progresses. Keep figures and tables tied to claims. Define symbols, show averages when discussed, report effect sizes, failures and limits.
  8. Validate and submit. Independently inspect code, metrics, citations and proofs; reproduce decisive results; follow venue-specific AI rules; obtain explicit human submission approval.

Good abstract skeleton

Problem: What specific failure matters? Gap: Why do existing methods fail? Method: What is new? Evidence: What verified experiment or theorem supports the claim? Implication: What changes for the field? Never add invented performance numbers.

Writing example

Using OCR Heads to Verbalize Image Semantics is a useful example of a narrow question expanding into an interpretable mechanism: locate relevant heads, use controlled interventions, build a lens, measure localization, and demonstrate an intervention. The causal evidence matters more than polished prose.

AI-use and submission integrity

Different venues allow very different levels of research or writing automation. Never assume a generic AI-writing policy applies across conferences. No fabricated results, invented citations, salami-sliced duplicate papers, or dual submissions.
